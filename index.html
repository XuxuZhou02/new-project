portfolio.home.approach.title

How I work

Start from the person's moment

Where are they, what are they doing, and how much attention do they have? A rider on a dark curb reads differently from a driver parked between trips.

Name it once

Every concept gets one name and one definition. The UI, help center, and training borrow it, so people never have to wonder whether two words mean the same thing.

Write for the hardest case

Copy should still work for someone who is late, stressed, using a screen reader, or reading in their second language. If it works there, it works everywhere.

Decide how to measure before writing

Fewer support contacts, faster recovery from errors, more new drivers reaching their first trip. Content is a product decision, so I tie it to a result.

One concept, every surface

A content model for a rideshare app that treats each concept, like a cancellation fee, as one connected object. When the policy changes, every surface that mentions it can change together.

Problem

What I made

A four-layer content model, a faceted taxonomy, a naming convention for string keys, and a publishing workflow built around them.

case.content_system.process

Process

  1. AuditTraced one concept, the cancellation fee, through six surfaces. Logged every name, rule, and owner I could find.
  2. DefineWrote a canonical definition and a list of names to avoid, so the concept has exactly one name.
  3. ModelSeparated facts from copy, and copy from placement. Specified the fields for each content type.
  4. StructureDesigned facets so content can be found from more than one path, and a key convention for the string library.
  5. OperationalizeRedesigned the publishing workflow and wrote governance rules so the model stays accurate over time.

case.content_system.problem

A single policy lives in many places

A rider cancels after their driver has already started driving to them. In a typical rideshare product, that moment touches a confirmation sheet, a push notification, a receipt line, a help center article, a driver training module, and the scripts support agents use.

Each of these is usually owned by a different team and stored in a different tool. Nothing connects them except the people who remember they exist. So when the policy changes, someone has to search for every mention by hand.

Audit: one concept, four names

I mapped how a single concept could drift across surfaces. These strings are written for this case study to show common patterns. They are not quotes from a real product.

SurfaceWhat it saysIssue
Confirmation sheetA fee may apply.Vague. Doesn't say when or how much.
Push notificationYou were charged a late cancel fee.New name. "Late cancel fee" appears nowhere else.
Help articleRiders who cancel after 5 minutes may be charged.Outdated. The current rule is 2 minutes.
Driver trainingYou'll receive a cancellation payment.Third name. Drivers and riders can't compare notes.

The problem wasn't any single string. There was no shared source for what a cancellation fee is, so every writer had to work it out again.

case.content_system.model

A content model in four layers

I separated what is true from how we say it and where we say it. Policy facts live in one place. Surface copy references them instead of retyping them.

Layer 1

Concept

The single source for what something is and who owns it.

  • canonical_name
  • definition
  • audience
  • policy_owner
  • review_date
Layer 2

Facts

Values that come from policy or config. Nobody types them by hand.

  • {fee_amount}
  • {grace_period}
Layer 3

Variants

Copy written for each surface, with its own length limit and tone.

  • ui.confirm_sheet
  • push
  • receipt
  • help.article
  • learning.lesson
  • support.macro
Layer 4

Placements

Where and when a variant appears: a screen, a trigger, an article ID.

  • screen_id
  • trigger_event
  • locale

case.content_system.naming

A naming convention people can read

String keys are the address of every piece of copy. A key that describes its location and purpose makes strings searchable, keeps duplicates out, and tells translators where the words will appear.

rideraudience. pickupstage. cancel_feeconcept ID. confirm_sheetcomponent. bodyelement
BeforecancelPopupTxt2
Afterrider.pickup.cancel_fee.confirm_sheet.body
  • Lowercase, with dots between levels and underscores inside a level.
  • The concept segment always matches a concept ID, so every string links back to its source.
  • Keys describe purpose, never the words on screen. btn_ok breaks as soon as the button says "Got it".
case.content_system.decisions

Decisions and trade-offs

Variables for facts, people for wording

Considered
Fully templated copy, generated from the concept.
Chose
Facts are variables. Each surface's sentence is written by a person.
Why
Templates keep numbers accurate but read like a robot wrote them, and a push notification needs different words than a help article. Variables solve accuracy. Writers handle tone.

Concepts match how people think

Considered
One concept per screen, or one per backend event.
Chose
One concept per idea a rider or driver would recognize.
Why
People talk about a "cancellation fee". Nobody talks about "trip state cancel post-accept", and modeling by event would split one idea into five.

One concept end to end, first

Considered
Designing the full taxonomy before modeling anything.
Chose
Model one concept across every surface, then generalize.
Why
A working example exposes edge cases a diagram hides. Modeling the support macro showed that agents also need a refund rule.
case.content_system.workflow

Rethinking the publishing workflow

A model only helps if the way teams publish uses it. I mapped how a policy change typically moves today and redesigned it around the concept object.

Before: search and hope

  1. Policy changes in a doc.Policy team
  2. Someone posts the change in a team chat.Product manager
  3. Each team searches its own tool for mentions.Design, help center, training, support
  4. Updates ship on different dates.Everyone
  5. Riders and drivers see conflicting rules until someone notices.Support

After: change the source

  1. Policy owner updates the fact on the concept.Policy team
  2. The system lists every variant that uses it.Content system
  3. Content designer reviews only the variants whose wording needs to change. The rest update automatically.Content design
  4. Localization gets one batch with full context.Localization
  5. All surfaces publish together, help center and training included.Engineering

Governance that keeps it working

  • A concept can't be published without an owner and a review date.
  • Every quarter, review the 20 concepts that drive the most support contacts.
  • New strings must reuse an existing concept or propose a new one. No orphans.
  • The model maps directly onto tools like Ditto (components and variables) and Contentful (content types and references).
case.content_system.localization_ai

Localization and AI support

Localization

  • Translators get the concept definition and a translator note with every string, so "fee" isn't translated three ways.
  • Facts are formatted by locale: $5.00 in en-CA, 5,00 $ in fr-CA.
  • Each concept name has one approved translation in the glossary, such as "frais d'annulation".

Where AI helps, with a person in charge

  • Drift checks: flag strings that use an avoided term or contradict a fact.
  • First drafts: suggest surface variants from the definition, within each surface's length limit.
  • Migration: find legacy strings that probably belong to a concept but aren't linked yet.
  • Never automatic: AI suggests and a content designer approves. Policy copy is never published without review.

Risks and how I'd reduce them

  • Teams keep writing outside the system. Make the library the fastest way to find and reuse copy, not an extra step.
  • Too many concepts too soon. A concept qualifies only when it appears on two or more surfaces.
  • Owners move on. Review dates and a quarterly ownership check catch concepts nobody is looking after.

  • ClaritySupport contacts tagged to a concept in the 4 weeks after a change.
case.content_system.reflection

What I learned

Start small

I'd pilot with the 20 concepts behind the most support contacts instead of migrating everything. Early wins make the case for the rest.

IA is a people problem

A naming convention only sticks if the people writing strings help shape it. Next time I'd run a card sort with designers and engineers before proposing the key structure.

When the ride doesn't go to plan

Error messages and alerts in a ride app are read at the worst possible time: on a curb, in the rain, with 8% battery. I rewrote six of them so riders always know what to do next.

Role
UX writer (solo)
Type
Self-initiated concept project
Timeline
[2 weeks, Fall 2026]
Tools
Figma, Hemingway Editor, VoiceOver

Problem

Generic errors like "Something went wrong" leave riders guessing, so they retry blindly, give up, or contact support.

What I made

A three-question framework for error copy, a tone scale tied to stakes, and six before-and-after rewrites with length limits and accessibility notes.

How I'd measure it

How many riders recover from each error on their own, and support contacts per 1,000 trips for each message.

case.error_states.process

Process

  1. MapWalked through a ride from request to receipt and listed every moment that can fail.
  2. PrioritizeScored each moment by how often it happens and how stressful it is for the rider.
  3. FrameWrote a three-question framework, a tone scale, and a component spec for error messages.
  4. Write and iterateDrafted each message several times and critiqued every draft against the framework.
  5. ReviewChecked length limits, screen reader output, reading level, and French text expansion.
  6. Hand off and testMapped error reasons to messages for engineering and planned a comprehension test.

case.error_states.framework

Every message answers three questions

  1. What happened?State the outcome in plain words. Lead with it.
  2. What does it mean for me?Am I charged? Is my ride still on? Answer the worry behind the error.
  3. What can I do now?One clear action, with a button that names it.

Rules I wrote for myself

  • No blame. "This card was declined", not "You entered an invalid card".
  • Save "sorry" for when we caused it. An apology on every message stops meaning anything.
  • Specific beats vague. "Try again in a few minutes" instead of "later". "Oct 31" instead of "expired".
  • Buttons say what happens. "Change payment", never "OK".
  • Keep error codes for support. Log them; don't show them to riders.
case.error_states.tone

One voice, tone set by the stakes

The voice stays the same: clear, calm, and human. The tone moves with the stakes. The more serious the moment, the less personality and the more precision.

Routine

Light and brief. The rider just needs one fact.

"Jordan is here. Look for a grey Corolla."

Friction

Calm and direct. Solution before explanation.

"Your card was declined. Choose another card to request your ride."

Safety

Plain and serious. No jokes, no brand flourishes.

"Your ride stopped unexpectedly. Are you OK?"

case.error_states.iteration

One message, four drafts

Most of the work happens between the first draft and the last. Here's how the payment declined message changed, and what each critique taught me.

  1. Draft 1
    Payment failedPlease update your payment method.

    Says what to do, but not why. Riders may think the app is broken and keep retrying.

  2. Draft 2
    Your card was declinedSorry! Your payment didn't go through. Please update your payment method and try again.

    Clearer cause. But "Sorry!" adds length without helping, and "try again" invites a retry with the same card.

  3. Draft 3
    Your card was declinedYour bank didn't approve this payment. Update your payment method.

    Naming the bank tells riders where the problem is. But "payment method" is system language. Riders think in cards.

  4. Final
    Your card was declinedYour bank didn't approve this payment. Choose another card or add a new one to request your ride.

    Uses riders' words, offers two concrete fixes, and ends with their goal: getting the ride.

case.error_states.accessibility

Accessible by default

  • Errors use words and an icon, never color alone.
  • Status changes are announced to screen readers with live regions: polite for driver updates, assertive only for safety alerts.
  • Button labels make sense out of context, because screen reader users often navigate by buttons alone.
  • Abbreviations get full accessible labels: "2 min" is read as "2 minutes", "Dr." is never used for "Drive".
  • Plain language, aiming for a grade 6 to 8 reading level, checked in Hemingway Editor.
  • Translation-ready: no idioms like "hang tight", and room for French text to run about 30% longer, which matters for Canadian French.
case.error_states.test_plan

Testing plan

Setup

  • Participants: 5 people who have used a rideshare app in the past month.
  • Method: unmoderated test on a phone mockup. Each person sees the before or after version of each message, for 5 seconds.
  • Order: versions are rotated so nobody sees both versions of the same message.

Questions and success criteria

  1. What happened?
  2. Will you be charged?
  3. What would you do next?
  4. How confident are you, from 1 to 5?

Success: at least 4 of 5 people answer "What would you do next?" correctly after one read, and confidence is higher than for the before version.

case.error_states.metrics

How I'd measure success

  • Recovery rateRiders who fix a declined payment and request a ride within 5 minutes.
  • Support contactsContacts per 1,000 trips that follow each message.
  • Next-step clarityIn a 5-person comprehension test, can people say what they'd do next after reading the message once?
  • Path choiceWhen no drivers are nearby, how many riders schedule a ride instead of leaving?
case.error_states.reflection

What I learned

The best error is one that doesn't happen

Two of these messages point to product fixes: warning riders about an expired card before they request, and setting pickup spots before the driver arrives. I'd bring those to design as proposals.

Shorter isn't always clearer

"Invalid code" is shorter than my rewrite, but it creates more work. I now count the reader's effort, not just the characters.

Ready for trip one

A learning path for new rideshare drivers that teaches each skill right before they need it, instead of front-loading everything before the first trip.

Role
Learning experience designer (solo)
Type
Self-initiated concept project
Timeline
[3 weeks, Fall 2026]
Tools
Figma, Google Docs, Miro

Problem

New drivers learn most things before their first trip, when they have the least context. By their first busy pickup, last week's video is forgotten.

What I made

Learning objectives, a four-stage path that splits "know before you drive" from "learn when it happens", and a sample micro-lesson with a knowledge check.

How I'd measure it

Time from sign-up to first trip, and support contacts from drivers during their first 10 trips.

On this page ProcessLearnersFirst weekLearning pathLesson outlinesSample lessonVideo scriptHelp articleEvaluation
case.driver_learning.process

Process

I followed the ADDIE model, with an extra step to keep revising after launch.

  1. AnalyzeDefined the learners, their constraints, and what they need to do in their first week.
  2. DesignWrote learning objectives and sorted every topic by when it matters.
  3. DevelopOutlined lessons, wrote a video script, a knowledge check, and a help article.
  4. ImplementPlanned where each piece appears: before driving, in context, or in the learning center.
  5. EvaluateBuilt an evaluation plan across four levels, from reaction to business results.
  6. ReviseUpdate lessons when pass rates or support contacts show that something is confusing.
case.driver_learning.audience

Who's learning, and when

My starting assumptions, which I'd validate with interviews before building:

  • Many new drivers drive part-time and learn on their phone, often parked between other commitments.
  • Many speak English as an additional language, so plain wording and visuals matter.
  • They are motivated by their first earnings, not by finishing a course.
  • They cannot read or tap anything while driving. Learning must happen when the car is stopped.

Learning objectives

Each objective names something a driver can do, so it can be checked.

After their first week, a new driver can:

  1. Confirm they have the right rider before starting a trip.
  2. Choose a safe, legal place to stop when the pickup pin is in a busy spot.
  3. Find their earnings for a trip and explain what each line means.
  4. Know what to do when a rider cancels or doesn't show up.
  5. Find and use the in-app safety tools.

case.driver_learning.sample_lesson

Sample micro-lesson: busy pickups

A two-minute lesson follows the same four beats every time, so drivers know what to expect.

  1. Frame 1 · Hook

    Start with a real moment

    The pin is on King St. There's nowhere to stop.What would you do?

    A scenario, not a rule. It makes the problem feel familiar before the lesson starts.

  2. Frame 2 · Teach

    Three rules, one screen

    1. Never stop in a bike lane or bus stop.2. Find the nearest legal spot.3. Tell your rider where you are.

    Short enough to remember. Illustrated so it works without reading every word.

  3. Frame 3 · Practice

    Check understanding

    A one-question scenario with feedback for every answer. Try it below.

    Every answer explains why, including the right one.

  4. Frame 4 · Apply

    Show up again at the right moment

    Busy pickup aheadFind a legal spot nearby, then message your rider.

    The tip appears when the car is stopped near a busy pin. Learning connects to the real task.

Your rider's pin is on a busy downtown street with no place to stop. What's the best next step?

help.driver.pickup_spot.article

Driver help › Pickups

Where can I stop for a pickup?

Stop at the nearest legal spot to the pin, then message your rider where you are. Never stop in a bike lane, at a bus stop, or in a fire route.

If you can't stop at the pin

  1. Drive past slowly and look for a legal spot within a short walk.
  2. Once you've stopped, tap Message and tell your rider where you are. For example: "I'm at the corner of King and John."
  3. Wait for your rider. Your wait time starts when you arrive near the pin.

At airports and event venues

Some places have marked pickup zones. As you get close, the app shows the zone and how to get there. Follow the zone instead of the pin.

Connected to the content system

case.driver_learning.inclusion

Safe and inclusive by design

  • In-context tips appear only when the car is stopped. Nothing asks for attention while driving.
  • Every video has captions and a transcript. Every lesson works with the sound off.
  • Plain language and illustrations support drivers reading in a second language.
  • Available in English and French at launch.
  • Lessons can be skipped and found later in the learning center, so experienced drivers aren't held back.
case.driver_learning.evaluation

Evaluation plan

I used the four levels of the Kirkpatrick model, so the evaluation looks past completion rates to whether drivers actually do things differently.

  • Level 1 · ReactionA one-tap "Was this useful?" after each lesson and tip, with an optional comment.
  • Level 2 · LearningFirst-try pass rate for each question, to find confusing content rather than just counting who passed.
  • Level 3 · BehaviorFewer unsafe-stop reports and fewer "couldn't find my driver" contacts during the first 10 trips.
  • Level 4 · ResultsDays from approval to first trip, early support contacts, and 30-day new driver retention.
case.driver_learning.reflection

What I learned

Less up front is a design decision

Removing content felt risky. Sorting by "when does this matter?" gave me a clear reason for every cut, which is what I'd need to convince stakeholders.

Next: talk to drivers

My audience profile is built on assumptions. Before building, I'd interview 5 to 8 newer drivers about their first week and what surprised them.
